why roon exists

You are taking more photographs than any generation in history — and losing more memories.

The camera roll was supposed to solve this. It didn't. It gave us volume without meaning: thousands of images nobody will ever open, filed under a date that tells you nothing about what actually happened that day.

Google Photos and iCloud keep your files. That's storage, and they're good at it. Roon does the part they don't: it turns what's in those files into your family's story. Roon isn't storage. It's the part nobody built — the part that remembers why it mattered.

what you can keep

A memory is never just a photograph.

It's the sound of her voice at four. The thing you scribbled at 2am because you didn't want to forget it. The video nobody meant to take. Roon keeps all of it in one place, in whatever form it arrived.

Photographs

Drop in one or ten thousand. Full resolution, never compressed, always yours to take back out.

Video

The clips that actually move — the wobble, the laugh, the eleven seconds before they noticed the camera.

Voice & audio

Record straight into Roon, or send in voice memos you already made. Voices change faster than faces.

Writing & journals

Type it here, or bring in notes you've kept elsewhere. One line counts. So does a thousand words at midnight.

Or connect what you already have — and stay in control of it.

Link Google Photos or Apple Photos and Roon will quietly surface what's worth keeping. Nothing is imported automatically and nothing is ever pulled behind your back.

You choose what crosses over. Approve a moment, a day, or a whole trip. Disconnect any time and everything you've kept stays exactly where it is.

where the name came from

Roon is named after my daughter, Rooney.

When she was four, she drew a little dinosaur. Wild spikes down its back, a lopsided smile, arms that don't quite reach anything. It's the logo you've been looking at this entire time. I have it tattooed on me.

She drew this at four years old. We didn't redraw it, clean it up, or hand it to a designer. It's hers, exactly as she made it — and it's the whole company's identity.

Rooney has a rare autoimmune disease. Her own immune system attacks her muscles. There's no cure. She has monthly infusions, chemotherapy, and steroids — a schedule that would flatten most adults, and she's a kid.

She also lives more in a week than most people manage in a year.

Watching her taught me something I wish I'd learned a gentler way: how fast this goes, and how much of it slips past while you're just trying to get through the day. The days you'd give anything to have back are almost never the ones you thought to photograph.

I wanted to build something that gives those days back — so she can live them all over again, and so we can too.

That's Roon. It exists for her first, and then for anyone who has ever realized, too late, what they'd stopped paying attention to. Whether you're keeping this for a newborn, a parent who won't be here forever, or for yourself — the reason is the same.

— Jeff, Rooney's dad

meet rooney

The storyteller inside Roon.

A quick note on the name: Rooney is my daughter. Rooney is also what we named the storyteller inside the app, because it does the one thing she taught us to do — pay attention to the small stuff. When you see Rooney in the product, that's the assistant. The girl came first.

It notices patterns

Across months and years, it sees what keeps repeating in your life that you're too close to spot.

“Dinosaurs found their way into almost everything she loved for three summers running.”

It writes the story

Not a caption, not a slideshow, not an album. Real chapters, with names, written in sentences from your own photos, words, and voice — a few honest paragraphs about what a day actually was.

“This was one of the happiest summers — and then, without much warning, so was the deep end of the pool.”

It asks what you'd never think to record

Gentle, specific questions — never a form, never a daily nag. The ones your kids will want answered in thirty years.

“You've never told us how she got her nickname.”

It builds collections on its own

You never make an album. Chapters assemble themselves, and they're named like memories, not folders.

“The Year She Wouldn't Come Inside” · 142 moments

It makes the year into a film

Every January, a cinematic look back — the happiest moments, the hardest ones, who mattered most. Not statistics.

Ends with one question, in your own voice: “What do you hope you'll remember when you're eighty?”

It holds letters until the right day

Write to someone now, choose when they receive it — a birthday, a wedding, a day decades from here.

“Your dad left this for today.”

What Rooney will never do

It will never invent a memory. Every word comes from something you gave it. If it doesn't know, it asks — it doesn't fill the gap.

It will never impersonate someone. Roon preserves the people you love. It does not simulate them, generate their voice, or pretend to speak as them. That line will not move.

Your memories stay yours. Never sold, never used to train public models, and you can export everything — originals included — any time.
